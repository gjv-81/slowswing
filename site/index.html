Our approach

The edge isn't predicting the market. It's conviction.

We built a proprietary setup using machine learning on years of market history — trained to recognize the conditions that have tended to precede strong swing-trade moves. On top of it sits a quality gate grounded in published academic research, so every name we surface isn't just technically interesting; it's a financially sound business you can stand behind.

Because markets are unpredictable. What separates successful traders from everyone else isn't forecasting the next move — it's how they act when things get uncomfortable. That's what we're really offering: conviction. When one of these high-quality setups runs into volatility, the quality gate is your reason to stay patient — sound companies tend to bounce back, even if it takes a longer holding period to get there.

We trained a machine-learning model on ten years of daily data across the U.S. market, and asked it a single question: which price behaviors best predicted which companies would outperform over the following few months? Market regime & timing

The tide matters as much as the boat

No stock trades in a vacuum. When the broad market — measured by SPY — is healthy and trending, most quality setups have the wind at their back. When it's weak or stretched, even a good setup fights a headwind. That's why the board carries a simple market regime read at the top: not to predict the market, but to tell you how hard to lean in today.

β

Beta

How much a stock amplifies the market's move. A beta near 1.5 tends to swing about 1.5× SPY — a tailwind in a strong tape, and painful in a weak one. High-beta names need a healthier regime to be worth the ride.

🔗

Correlation to SPY

How tightly a name tracks the market. Highly correlated names give you little shelter — when SPY drops, they usually drop with it. Lower-correlation quality names are steadier when the tape turns.

🎚️

The regime dial

A four-step read on how stretched the S&P 500 is versus its own recent history: Green (recently reset — historically the friendliest backdrop), Yellow (moderately stretched, an ordinary reading), Orange (unusually stretched — most such periods pass quietly, but the market's rare deep drops have historically started here), Red (below its long-term trend). Context for how hard to lean in — never a forecast or a command.

How to use it — with the numbers

In our ten-year research, the same setups returned on average +8.6% over twelve weeks when entered with the dial Red (the market below its long-term trend, after a correction) against +1.7% when entered in Yellow (stretched). That is roughly a five-fold difference from the same stock list — the dial is a return lever, not just a mood indicator.

The live book, started in July 2026, says the same thing so far: setups added with the market Healthy averaged +7.0% at four weeks (90 setups) against −0.4% for those added with it Extended (11 setups — a small sample, read with care).

So, in practice: Green or Red — the board has usually earned fuller attention and the more patient, bigger Phoenix names tend to pay; Yellow — ordinary, size as you normally would; Orange — be choosier, smaller, and favour the steadier Cruise names. The dial changes how much, never whether the setup is real.

We frame regime as context, not a command — a way to gauge how supportive conditions are, never a hard veto or a market forecast. We can't predict the market any more than we can predict a single stock. A weak regime is generally a reason for caution — the environment tends to favor lower-beta, less-correlated quality names; a healthy regime is more supportive. The quality gate

The reason you can hold through a dip

The hardest part of swing trading isn't finding a setup — it's holding it when it goes against you for a while. Most people sell good positions at the worst possible moment out of fear. Our fix is upstream: every name we publish first has to clear a fundamental safety screen. So when a position wobbles, you already know the business behind it is solid. That's what lets you sit still.

  • Profitability & earnings quality — is the company genuinely making money, cleanly?
  • Financial health — a multi-point accounting-strength check on the balance sheet.
  • Distress screen — a hard veto on names with real bankruptcy risk. They never reach your board.
  • Self-funding — does the business generate its own cash, or lean on debt?

Screens are grounded in published research (Piotroski, Altman, Novy-Marx, Sloan). The gate's job isn't to boost returns — it's to keep fragile businesses out, so a temporary drawdown stays temporary.

01 · Methodology & discipline

A method you actually follow beats a better method you don't

Every trader — winning or losing — has a methodology. What separates the successful ones isn't a cleverer secret; it's the discipline to follow their method consistently, without improvising the moment emotions flare. A repeatable process quietly removes the thousand small, emotional decisions that erode returns over time.

That's where The Slow Swing fits. It's a disciplined, unemotional filter that runs the same way every single night. Use it as your first filter — a vetted shortlist to begin your own research from — or as a second opinion after your own screen. Either way, it anchors your process in something consistent, instead of a gut feeling that changes with your mood.

02 · Emotional control

Winners are made in the uncomfortable middle

The biggest difference between a winning trade and a losing one is rarely the entry — it's the grit to hold through the uncomfortable stretch in between. Nobody can predict the next move. There are simply too many variables acting on a stock at once for anyone to know which way it goes next, and pretending otherwise is exactly how people get shaken out at the worst possible moment.

What carries you through those stretches is conviction — and conviction has to be earned, not faked. When you genuinely believe in the business underneath (which is precisely what the quality gate is for), a deep drawdown becomes something you can sit through, and even average into deliberately, rather than panic-sell. Markets routinely overreact — over-punishing a strong company on a small revenue miss — and those are often the names that rebound fastest once sentiment comes back to its senses.

There's a subtler trap, too: scale changes how a loss feels, even when the math doesn't. Early on, a $100 loss on a $1,000 trade barely registers — but a $10,000 loss on a $100,000 trade can throw you into internal chaos, or freeze you from making the next trade at all. On a percentage basis they're identical; our brains simply aren't wired to see it that way — we react to the dollar figure, not the ratio. The skill to build is getting to the point where both losses feel the same. Traders who master this don't get rattled out of a sound approach early, before it's had the chance to work — which is often the whole difference between winning and losing over time.

Conviction is not stubbornness. It's grounded in the company's quality — not in a refusal to ever be wrong. If the reason you owned it genuinely breaks, that's a different situation from ordinary volatility.

02½ · The chop

The chop is the toll, not a signal

Your stock runs up 12%. Then it gives half of it back in a week. The chart looks sick. Everything in you says: get out while you're still green.

We wanted to build a warning light for exactly that moment. So we went back through 9,000 of our past signals and asked one question: when a stock starts looking sick mid-trade, does it actually do worse afterward?

No. It doesn't. The "sick-looking" stocks did just as well over the next two months as the healthy-looking ones. The ugly dip told us nothing about what came next. We tried three different warning lights. All three failed the test.

That's why there is no "take profits now" light on this board. Not because we're hiding one — because we looked, and it doesn't exist.

Here's the honest math on those scary dips. Out of every 100 of them, about 50 climbed all the way back. About 50 didn't. A coin flip — and nothing we tested could tell you which side your stock is on. But the winners gained more than the losers lost, so the group as a whole still made good money. Sell every dip out of fear, and you dump the 50 winners along with the 50 losers — and lose more than you save.

So think of the dip as a toll booth. It's the price the market charges for the full move. You can't drive around it. You can only make sure the toll never breaks you — and that's done before the trade, not during it: keep every position small enough that a bad dip doesn't matter. Small positions, no panic. That's the whole trick.

3 · The life of a setup

Active, Out of Range, Retired

A name stays on the live board until its move has happened and four weeks have passed since it was added; a name whose move never comes leaves at twelve weeks. Three stages, and the data has something to say about each.

🟢

Active

Has not yet traded 5% above its added price (its Peak is under 5%) — or is below it. The setup is, in our terms, still ripe, and it stays Active for up to twelve weeks. Price decides this, not the calendar: a name can be Active in week nine. Names that are down from where they were added are still Active on purpose: in our data, entries near or below the line did better than entries after the move had started.

📈

Out of Range

Has traded 5% or more above its added price at least once — its Peak is 5% or more. The move has happened. We keep these visible so you can see how setups play out — not as an invitation to chase. Chasing is the single easiest way to turn a good setup into a bad trade.

📁

Retired

Once a name has run 5% and is four weeks past being added — or twelve weeks after being added if it never runs — it leaves the board and lands on the Track Record with its real returns, its peak and its worst dip. Every name retires — winners, losers, and the ones that went nowhere. Nothing is quietly dropped.

Why the move decides, not the calendar. The setups are built around a twelve-week horizon, and the board used to drop every name at four weeks. When we tested that on 4,865 historical signals, it was the wrong ruler: a name that was still near its added price at week four went on to return about 6% over the following eight weeks on average, and names that were down more than 10% at week four did best of all — while names that had already run 5%+ by week four paid the least from there. The median low on the path comes around day five, and roughly one in five eventual +25% winners dipped more than 20% on the way. Dropping a name at four weeks was grading it at its statistically ugliest moment. So since Oct 7, 2026 a name stays Active until it has actually run; once it has, and the four-week mark has passed, it retires. This is a reporting window, not a holding rule.

What retirement doesn't mean. A retired name is not a sell signal, any more than an Active name is a buy signal. The board tracks a window; what you do inside or beyond it is yours.

The evening routine we use ourselves

The board refreshes after each close. This is how we read it — once, in the evening, in about ten minutes. Described, not prescribed.

  1. The regime bar first. SPY and QQQ against their own history. Green means the market has recently reset; yellow, extended but ordinary; orange, stretched. In our data the same setups did several times better entered after a reset than entered stretched. This is context for how bold to be, never a trigger.
  2. New names, then the 💎 filter. Anything with a NEW badge qualified this week. Collapse to High-Quality only. The gate exists so that when a name dips — and nearly all of them dip — you're holding a business sound enough to sit through it.
  3. Phoenix or Cruise, and the AI Score. Phoenix is the bigger, slower move that tests you harder on the way; Cruise is the smaller, quicker one. The score is conviction: how strongly the conditions matched. Higher is better; it is not a forecast.
  4. Pulse @ Add and Pulse now. How the name was moving against the market when it qualified, and how it is moving tonight. A tie-breaker between names, not a reason on its own.
  5. Click the ticker. The shape of the move since the stock was added — peak, worst dip, where it is now — as percentages. This is where you check whether a name is still below, at or just above its added price, or has already run.
  6. What we ignore. The intraday chart. The news. Anything that happened before tonight's close. The board is built on daily closes, and reading it more than once a day adds noise, not information.

Then close the tab. A few deliberate looks a week is the point of the product. If you find yourself checking hourly, you are reading it wrong — the psychology page is about why.

5 · What we tested and rejected

Three studies, one conclusion

Most swing-trading playbooks come with the same three tools: a stop-loss, a profit target, and a rule for getting out when the trade "looks wrong." We tested each of them on our own signals — thousands of them, across years — before deciding whether to build them into the product. All three failed.

🛑

Stop-losses

Every stop level we tested, from tight to loose, reduced returns. The reason is on the psychology page: nearly every winner dips first, and a stop sells the dip.

🎯

Profit targets

Taking gains at a fixed level cut the expectancy of the whole book. The setups' edge comes from the minority that keep running, and a target sells exactly those.

🌧️

Warning lights

Three definitions of "conditions are changing" mid-trade, tested on 8,974 signals. None predicted weaker returns ahead. The chop is the toll.

The conclusion we drew for ourselves: at this horizon, risk control that happens during a trade — stops, targets, exit signals — cost more than it saved. The risk control that survived testing happens before the trade: position sizes small enough that a bad dip, or a total loss, does not matter. That is why the board carries no exits, no targets and no warning lights, and why the psychology page keeps returning to sizing.

One more finding that surprised us. The model's score tends to fall after a setup works. That is not the setup deteriorating; it is the markdown closing — the very thing the model was looking for going away because the stock recovered. A falling score after entry is what winning looks like in this system.

All of this is hypothetical, backtested research (2019–2026, no transaction costs, survivorship bias applies). It describes what we found and what we chose not to build. It is not a recommendation to trade without stops, or to hold anything — every exit is a personal decision with real risk of loss.

8 · Reading the track record honestly

What the numbers are, and what they aren't

The Track Record shows every retired setup, winners and losers alike. Four things to hold in mind while reading it:

  • Peak is not a return. "Touched +10%" means the price reached that level at its best point. Nobody sells the exact top. The two-, four- and twelve-week columns are real close-to-close figures; the peak is a ceiling, not an outcome.
  • It's paper. Every name is tracked from the day it was added at one share, with no costs, no slippage and no exits. It measures the setup, not a trader.
  • Survivorship bias applies. The model was built on today's stocks — the ones that survived to be listed. That flatters any backtest, ours included. The live book, running since July 2026, is the honest test, and it is still young.
  • The headline is a monthly snapshot. The big numbers on the home page are frozen on the last trading day of each month and never restated. The Track Record page recomputes nightly, so the two can differ by a point or two. That is deliberate — a headline shouldn't wobble daily.

FAQ

The seven steps are on Getting started; every term on the board is one line each in the Handbook glossary. This page is the short list.

Quick answers

Ten things worth knowing

Is a setup a buy signal?

It's a shortlist. A setup tells you a quality company is in a position that has historically been followed by a move — the hard, time-consuming part of finding candidates is done. Which ones you trade, and how, is where your judgement comes in.

How many setups a night is normal?

Usually two to six, sometimes zero. The model only adds a stock when the conditions are there. A long list would mean the bar is too low.

A stock is down 15% and still Active. Why?

Because it hasn't run yet — and our research says that's often where the opportunity is. Names still near or below their added price after a few weeks went on to do better than names that had already moved. So we keep them Active deliberately, so you don't lose sight of them. More →

Why did a name leave the board?

It retired: either it had run 5% and passed the four-week mark, or it spent twelve weeks without running. Retired names are on the Track Record with their real returns. Nothing is quietly dropped. More →

What does Pending mean?

The stock qualified in tonight's scan. Our paper book enters it at tomorrow's open — nobody could have bought at tonight's close — so there is no price or scorecard until then.

Why percentages and not prices?

The move is what matters for judging a setup, and a percentage makes a $60 stock and a $900 stock comparable on one board. The price on any given day is in your brokerage account. More →

What do the balloons on the home page mean?

Across every setup that completed its first four weeks, the share whose Peak reached +5% and +10% — set against the same measure for a typical S&P 500 stock. Snapshot taken at each month end and never restated. Hypothetical peak, not returns anyone earned. Month by month →

Why "trading, not investing"?

Because the setups describe a price condition that has tended to resolve over weeks — not a company to own for years. Different horizon, different mindset. More →

Why no stops or targets?

We tested them on thousands of our own setups; every version made results worse. The dip is usually the toll, not the verdict. More →

Is this advice? Can I ask about a particular stock?

It's research, and we're a publisher — the same board goes to every member. We don't know your situation, so we can't tell you what to buy or comment on individual trades. What we can do is make sure every name on the board has earned its place and show you exactly how each one has behaved.

This Privacy Policy explains what information Theslowswing LLC ("The Slow Swing," "we," "us") collects when you use the The Slow Swing website and subscription (the "Service"), how we use it, and the choices you have. By using the Service you agree to this policy.

Information we collect

  • Account information — the email address you sign up with, and any name or profile details you provide. Authentication is by email; we do not ask you to create a password we store.
  • Billing information — when you subscribe, payment is processed by our payment provider (Stripe). We receive limited details such as your subscription status and the last four digits and type of your card; we do not receive or store full card numbers. Your card data is handled by the processor under its own security and privacy terms.
  • Usage information — basic technical and usage data such as pages viewed, actions taken, device and browser type, and approximate location derived from your IP address, collected to operate and improve the Service.
  • Local storage — the site stores small items in your browser (for example, your logged-in state and whether you've seen the product tour) so the Service works as expected. This stays on your device.
  • Communications — if you email us, we keep your message and contact details to respond.

How we use information

  • to provide, maintain, and secure the Service and your account;
  • to process your subscription and billing through our payment provider;
  • to send service-related messages (for example, sign-in links, billing notices, and important changes);
  • to understand usage and improve the research, content, and experience; and
  • to comply with legal obligations and enforce our Terms of Service.

Because The Slow Swing publishes the same impersonal research to all subscribers, we do not build individualized investment profiles of you, and we do not use your data to tailor securities-related recommendations.

How we share information

We do not sell your personal information. We share it only with:

  • Legal and safety recipients when required by law, to respond to lawful requests, or to protect our rights, users, or the public; and
  • A successor in connection with a merger, acquisition, or sale of assets, subject to this policy.

Cookies and analytics

We (and our providers) may use cookies, local storage, and similar technologies to keep you signed in, remember preferences, and measure usage. You can control cookies through your browser settings; disabling some may affect how the Service works.

Security

We use reasonable administrative, technical, and organizational measures to protect your information. No method of transmission or storage is completely secure, however, and we cannot guarantee absolute security.
